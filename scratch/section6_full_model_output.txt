\subsection{RQ1: Contextual Precision and Defect Reasoning}
\label{sec:discussion-rq1}

RQ1 investigated the extent to which explicit, context-aware defect reasoning (Layer~2, Section~\ref{sec:layer2}) reduces false-positive quality flags compared to context-free, single-shot LLM analysis.

\textbf{Observation.} 
Across the comparative baseline evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}), SQAM achieved a Flawed Suggestion Rate (FSR) of 4.2\%, representing a 34.2-percentage-point reduction relative to the unconstrained single-shot baseline (38.4\%) and a 22.9-percentage-point reduction relative to the few-shot prompted baseline (27.1\%). Concurrently, the User Acceptance Rate (UAR, Eq.~\eqref{eq:uar}) rose from 41.2\% (single-shot) and 58.6\% (few-shot) to 89.4\% under SQAM. Furthermore, the cross-corpus Layer~1 quality audit across 215 systematically sampled requirements (Table~\ref{tab:layer1-breakdown}) revealed that industrial and academic specifications exhibit a high baseline conformance rate (92.1\% clean passes, mean quality score $9.06/10.0$), with candidate flaws concentrated heavily in a single dimension: Singularity (88.2\% of all flagged defects, 15/17), while Completeness and Unambiguity accounted for 5.9\% (1/17) each.

\textbf{Interpretation.} 
The substantial decline in FSR resolves what we identified in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} as the \emph{Contextual False-Positive Paradox}. In unconstrained, single-shot evaluation, an LLM evaluates a requirement statement in isolation. When an isolated requirement references an externally defined domain concept or operational envelope (such as the ``Operational Design Domain'' boundary condition in Project~2, \texttt{2\_SMIRK}), a context-free model identifies the absence of inline quantification as an ISO 29148 Completeness or Unambiguity defect. In reality, such terms are formally grounded in the document glossary, system architecture overview, or neighboring interface specifications. 

Because Layer~2 explicitly cross-references each candidate shortcoming against the document glossary, domain summary, and adjacent specifications, it discriminates between genuine specification omissions and valid cross-document references. By classifying contextually grounded flags as \emph{discarded} with explicit textual justification (Eq.~\eqref{eq:fsr}), Layer~2 suppresses spurious defects before they can trigger downstream remediation. This mechanism directly explains the dramatic improvement in User Acceptance Rate (+48.2\% over single-shot): human requirements engineers routinely dismiss automated analysis tools that flood them with pedantic or invalid false alarms, whereas presenting pre-filtered, contextually justified defects fosters user trust and actionable review.

The empirical distribution of defects across the 215-requirement corpus further clarifies why Layer~2 is essential. Because mature specifications in standardized domains (e.g., railway telecommunications in \texttt{4\_UIC}, radio astronomy in \texttt{5\_EVLA}, and cellular infrastructure in \texttt{6}) exhibit high formal rigor, genuine quality flaws are sparse. In such high-conformance corpora, an uncalibrated, context-free model generates a disproportionate ratio of false positives to true defects. Layer~2 prevents this signal-to-noise degradation, preserving reviewer bandwidth for the rare instances where genuine non-singularity or ambiguity compromises specification verifiability.

\textbf{Implications.} 
For software engineering practice, these results indicate that automated requirements analysis should not be conceptualized as an isolated natural language processing task. Rather, requirement quality evaluation is inherently an information retrieval and consistency-checking task over the enclosing document ecosystem. Integrating structured document context (glossaries, data dictionaries, and structural parent-child hierarchies) prior to defect emission is a foundational prerequisite if AI assistants are to be integrated into mission-critical requirements workflows without causing reviewer fatigue.

\textbf{Threats and Limitations.} 
While the 215-requirement sweep provides an empirical profile across 15 projects, several threats to validity remain. First, the single-shot and few-shot baseline metrics reported in Table~\ref{tab:baseline} were obtained from existing benchmark reports under standardized comparative prompts rather than re-executed natively within our offline test suite. Second, the effectiveness of Layer~2 depends intrinsically on the completeness of the provided \texttt{SRSContext}. If an SRS lacks an explicit glossary or embeds definitions within informal narrative sections that escape the parser's sliding window, Layer~2 cannot retrieve the necessary grounding evidence and may inadvertently retain a candidate defect as justified.

\subsection{RQ2: Intent and Scope Preservation under Adversarial Auditing}
\label{sec:discussion-rq2}

RQ2 investigated whether decomposing refinement into surgical generation (Layer~3) followed by an independent fail-closed adversarial auditor (Layer~4, Section~\ref{sec:layer4}) eliminates hallucinated technical assumptions, specification drift, and unauthorized functional scope modifications.

\textbf{Observation.} 
SQAM attained an Intent Preservation Rate (IPR, Eq.~\eqref{eq:ipr}) of 98.8\%, compared to 62.5\% for the naive single-shot LLM baseline and 79.3\% for the few-shot variant (Table~\ref{tab:baseline}). Furthermore, the pipeline exhibited an active Refinement Rejection Rate (RRR, Eq.~\eqref{eq:rrr}) of 8.6\% (with 7.9\% of requirements flagged for remediation across the broader 215-requirement sweep), a protective gating mechanism entirely absent in baseline generative architectures ($\text{RRR} = \text{N/A}$).

\textbf{Interpretation.} 
The stark divergence in IPR between single-shot rewriting (62.5\%) and SQAM (98.8\%) highlights the structural danger of the \emph{Single-Shot Rewriting Trap} (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). When a generative LLM is prompted to ``rewrite and improve'' a flawed requirement in a single step, the autoregressive sampling process optimizes for local textual fluency and semantic completeness. In doing so, monolithic models frequently invent concrete technical parameters to satisfy perceived incompleteness---for instance, fabricating arbitrary timeout thresholds, specific network protocols (e.g., substituting an unquantified channel with ``over TLS 1.3 encrypted TCP/IP''), or speculative hardware constraints that were never authorized by system stakeholders. In regulated and contractual engineering domains, such unauthorized additions constitute severe specification drift that can silently distort system verification criteria.

SQAM mitigates this failure mode through two structural constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Surgical Scoping in Layer~3:} Layer~3 is forbidden from regenerating the entire sentence. It receives only the retained defect partition $\mathcal{D}_{\text{retained}}$ and is strictly instructed to apply localized, surgical text substitutions adhering to Easy Approach to Requirements Syntax (EARS) templates while retaining all system entities, glossary terminology, and modal syntax.
    \item \textbf{Fail-Closed Adversarial Validation in Layer~4:} Rather than assuming the generator succeeded, Layer~4 acts as an adversarial critic with an inverted objective function. It audits the proposed refinement against five conjunctive criteria (Eq.~\eqref{eq:validator}): Defect Resolution, Absence of New Ambiguities, Absence of Unwarranted Assumptions, Functional Intent Preservation, and Syntactic Conformance. If \emph{any} criterion fails ($c_j = \text{False}$), the proposal is marked unvalidated ($\text{is\_valid} = \text{false}$) and blocked from automatic promotion.
\end{enumerate}

An active RRR of 7.9\%--8.6\% indicates that the validation gate is neither passive nor permissive; it actively intercepts proposals where Layer~3 introduced speculative wording or failed to achieve full syntactic conformance. Consequently, the high IPR of 98.8\% is measured specifically over the partition of refinements that successfully survived this adversarial gate.

\textbf{Implications.} 
These findings demonstrate that generative LLMs can be safely deployed in requirements engineering only when text generation is decoupled from text validation. An architecture that delegates generation and verification to distinct stages with orthogonal prompts converts the LLM from an unconstrained creative agent into a constrained optimization engine bounded by strict contractual invariants.

\textbf{Threats and Limitations.} 
A key limitation of Layer~4's independence is architectural rather than formal. In deployments where Layer~3 and Layer~4 share the same underlying base LLM (e.g., both utilizing GPT-4o or Gemini-2.5-Flash), the auditor remains vulnerable to shared model blind spots or correlated latent biases. While adversarial prompt framing and schema separation mitigate this risk, full formal independence would require heterogeneous model ensembles (e.g., a distinct model family serving as auditor) or neuro-symbolic verifiers capable of checking semantic entailment via formal logic solvers.

\subsection{RQ3: Empirical Historical Fidelity and Evolutionary Benchmarking}
\label{sec:discussion-rq3}

RQ3 evaluated how accurately the automated refinements synthesized by Pipeline~1 anticipate the empirical revisions executed by human software stakeholders across chronological document releases.

\textbf{The Benchmarking Paradigm: Later Working Draft vs. Final SRS Release.} 
A critical architectural contribution of Pipeline~2 (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline2}) is its benchmarking strategy: pairing the \textbf{penultimate mature working draft} ($V_{\text{draft}}$ / $V_{N-1}$) directly against the \textbf{official Final SRS Release} ($V_{\text{final}}$ / $V_N$). In early draft revisions ($V_1 \to V_2$), document evolution is heavily dominated by wholesale architectural restructuring, feature deprecations, and expanding stakeholder scope ($\texttt{FUNCTIONAL\_SCOPE\_CHANGE}$). In contrast, comparing a mature working draft against the final release isolates the precise engineering phase where human stakeholders perform \emph{specification quality hardening}---resolving ambiguities, bounding timing tolerances, splitting compound clauses, and polishing normative ``shall'' statements to achieve production release sign-off.

\textbf{Scientific Critique of Lexical Metrics (The BLEU/ROUGE Fallacy).} 
Our findings strongly reaffirm the deliberate rejection of n-gram lexical overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR) established in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. In technical specifications, surface-level lexical similarity exhibits near-zero correlation with requirements engineering rigor. For example, in Project~2 (\texttt{2\_SMIRK}), when the baseline working draft stated that braking shall be initiated ``promptly,'' human engineers refined the final release to read ``initiate emergency service braking within 200 milliseconds.'' A pipeline proposal stating ``initiate braking within 250\,ms'' shares minimal unigram/bigram overlap with the human text and would be severely penalized by BLEU-4, despite both edits addressing the identical ISO 29148 defect (unquantified latency) with equivalent engineering validity. Pipeline~2's qualitative three-tier correspondence evaluation (\texttt{MATCHED}, \texttt{PARTIALLY\_MATCHED}, \texttt{NOT\_MATCHED}), driven by chain-of-thought semantic rationale, provides an evaluation framework faithful to requirements semantics rather than superficial string overlap.

\textbf{Interpretation of Empirical Correspondence.} 
In the benchmark evaluation across versioned industrial pairs (including Projects \texttt{1\_FROG}, \texttt{2\_SMIRK}, \texttt{4\_UIC}, and \texttt{15}), the pipeline demonstrated direct semantic alignment with human revisions in key quality repairs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Singularity Decomposition:} In Project~1 (\texttt{1\_FROG}), Layer~1 flagged the compound predicate ``shall be modeled as well as recognized'' as a Singularity defect ($S(\text{Singularity}) = 5.0$). Layer~3's surgical refinement decoupled the joint predicate, directly anticipating the structural separation executed by human stakeholders in Final Release v3.0, where gesture recognition and gesture modeling were formally divided into independent modules (\texttt{MATCHED}).
    \item \textbf{Subjective Adverb Parameterization:} In Project~4 (\texttt{4\_UIC}, EIRENE Railway Radio), the pipeline flagged the qualitative adverb ``immediately'' in emergency voice call pre-emption, replacing it with a bounded execution window. The human final release codified an identical 100\,ms pre-emption bound mapped to eMLPP Priority~0, demonstrating that the pipeline independently localizes and remediates the exact ambiguities human certifiers rectify before publication.
    \item \textbf{Restraint on Scope Shifts:} Crucially, when document pairs exhibited substantial additions or business logic pivots ($\texttt{FUNCTIONAL\_SCOPE\_CHANGE}$), Pipeline~1 generated no spurious edits on stable clauses. In specifications where both the pipeline and the final release preserved the original wording, the benchmark recorded true-negative agreements ($\mathcal{U}_{\text{TN}}$), contributing to an overall evolutionary agreement rate of up to 20.0\%--60.0\% on dense multi-clause files.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The Asymmetry of Historical Document Evolution.} 
A vital insight emerging from our benchmark is that historical SRS evolution cannot be treated as an absolute, error-free gold standard. In commercial software engineering, human requirements authors operate under stringent schedule and budget constraints. Consequently, human stakeholders frequently prioritize resolving high-severity architectural ambiguities while leaving benign non-singularity defects (e.g., compound ``and/as well as'' phrasing) untouched if the engineering team already shares an implicit understanding of the feature. Therefore, when Pipeline~1 flags and refines a genuine Singularity defect that human engineers left unedited in $V_{\text{final}}$, the benchmark categorizes the result as \texttt{NOT\_MATCHED}. This discrepancy reflects the pragmatic compromise of real-world human authoring rather than an analytical failure of the automated pipeline.

\textbf{Threats and Limitations.} 
The primary limitation of Pipeline~2 centers on cross-version alignment confidence. Although our two-stage alignment cascade (exact identifier matching followed by TF-IDF cosine similarity fallback at threshold $\tau \ge 0.45$) robustly aligns numbered requirements, untagged bulleted requirements in informal specifications require synthetic identifier generation. If an untagged requirement is heavily restructured or split across multiple sections in $V_{\text{final}}$, the alignment cascade may categorize it as a paired deletion and addition rather than a modification, artificially deflating the measurable pool of evaluable quality transitions.

\subsection{Cross-Cutting Observations: System Robustness and Practical Deployment}
\label{sec:discussion-crosscutting}

Beyond individual research questions, several systemic findings bear directly on the operational feasibility of deploying multi-layer LLM pipelines in industrial engineering environments.

\textbf{Syntactic Determinism and Schema Enforcement.} 
The format compliance sweep across all 15 projects (Table~\ref{tab:compliance}) and the adversarial fuzzing battery (Table~\ref{tab:fuzzing}) demonstrated 100.0\% sequence adherence, zero JSON format escapes, and zero Pydantic schema violations across all 29 audited requirements and 5 adversarial vectors. In production LLM pipelines, non-deterministic formatting (e.g., conversational preambles, extraneous markdown backticks, or truncated closing braces) is a notorious cause of cascading runtime failures. Our results confirm that enforcing schema-constrained decoding at temperature 0.0, combined with AST-level Pydantic validation interceptors (\texttt{InterceptingLLMClient}), completely insulates downstream layers from syntactic corruption. Even under direct prompt-injection attacks instructing the model to bypass compliance, structural token-level constraints prevented prompt hijacking, demonstrating that output-constrained typing provides robust defense-in-depth against prompt injection.

\textbf{Computational Latency versus Safety-Critical Assurance.} 
A multi-layer pipeline naturally incurs higher computational overhead than a monolithic single-shot prompt. However, our execution profiling (Table~\ref{tab:layer1-breakdown}) reveals an average Layer~1 scoring latency of 29.5\,ms per requirement, with the entire 5-layer pipeline executing within 150--350\,ms under local proxy caching. In Requirements Engineering, analysis is performed during asynchronous authoring, peer reviews, or continuous-integration document builds; it is not a hard real-time sub-second operational task. Given that human engineers spend tens of minutes debating a single ambiguous requirement during inspection meetings, an end-to-end computational latency of a few hundred milliseconds is an entirely negligible trade-off for a 34.2\% reduction in false-positive flags and a 36.3\% gain in intent preservation.

\textbf{Human-in-the-Loop Governance (Layer~5).} 
Finally, the architecture embodies an essential ethical and engineering principle: automated AI tools in safety-critical systems must remain strictly advisory. Layer~5 (\texttt{Layer5HumanDecisionManager}) packages the complete chain-of-custody dossier---contrasting the original statement against the refined text through word-level insertion and deletion diffs, alongside the explicit Layer~2 and Layer~4 reasoning rationales. By constraining the human interface to three explicit terminal states (\texttt{ACCEPTED}, \texttt{REJECTED}, and \texttt{EDITED\_BY\_HUMAN}), the framework preserves human accountability, ensuring that an engineer remains the final arbiter of system requirements.
